# Research: honest sales craft and short video ads

Research round 2026-11. This file covers two things Studio writers and the video storyboard need
and do not have in one place yet:

- **Part 1:** how a piece sells without lying. It covers awareness stages, hooks, offers,
  objections, calls to action, proof and the mechanisms that explain why a piece works.
- **Part 2:** how a 9:16 video ad of 6 to 30 seconds is built. It covers arcs and timings, rules
  for AI video models and how a reviewer judges a draft from three frames and the script.

Ground rules carried from the sector files in this folder:

- Everything here is brand-neutral.
- Nothing is invented: no figures, reviews, chats, search results, press, awards, people
  presented as customers, or deadlines the business did not give.
- "Proof" means something the business can supply: its own work, its own numbers, a review it
  has permission to quote, a credential it holds, a guarantee it really offers.

Where a rule needs real data, it says so. In Mellox terms, that data comes from Brand DNA, the
product catalogue, the goal, the campaign brief or the person's own words. A rule with no data
behind it is skipped, never filled in.

All text is written in our own words. Sources are listed at the end. No prompt from a source is
copied: the model notes are paraphrased lessons.

# Part 1: Sales and persuasion that stays honest

## 1.1 Awareness stages: what each part of the piece must do

Eugene Schwartz's five levels of awareness (*Breakthrough Advertising*, 1966) still hold up. Where
the reader stands decides how much the opening line may assume. Misjudge the stage and the
piece fails:

- A product-name headline shown to someone who doesn't know they have the problem means nothing
  to them.
- A long problem story shown to a returning customer wastes their time.

Studio can infer the stage from the objective and the audience:

| Objective or audience | Likely stage |
|---|---|
| A cold awareness ad | unaware or problem aware |
| A retargeting ad | product aware |
| A post to existing followers about a sale | most aware |
| A launch | solution aware or product aware |

| Stage | What the reader knows | Headline must | Picture must | CTA must | Common mistake |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Unaware** | Nothing about the problem or the brand | Lead with a moment, scene or truth they recognise in their own life. No product name yet | Show the situation or the feeling, not the product. A person in the moment, a familiar mess, a relatable scene | Be soft and low-commitment: "see how", "watch", "save this" | Leading with features or the brand name |
| **Problem aware** | Feels the pain, doesn't know a fix exists | Name the problem in their own words, then hint there is a way out | Show the pain clearly and honestly: the leak, the clutter, the empty calendar, the confusing form | Point to learning the fix: "see the fix", "how we do it", "book a check" | Agitating so hard it shames or scares |
| **Solution aware** | Knows fixes exist, hasn't chosen one | Say why this approach beats the other kinds of fix: faster, simpler, safer, closer, made differently | Show the method or the difference: the process step, the material, the screen, the before-and-after of the work (where allowed) | Compare or try: "see the difference", "try it", "get a quote" | Naming or mocking a competitor (Mellox never names one) |
| **Product aware** | Knows this brand, isn't convinced | Remove the last doubt: proof, guarantee, detail, price clarity | Show the real product or real work close up, with the strongest honest proof (see 1.6) | Commit: "order", "book", "call", with a real condition if any | Repeating the pitch instead of answering the doubt |
| **Most aware** | Wants it, waiting for a reason or a reminder | State the offer plainly: what, price, when | Show the product or service with the offer and the date, big and clear | Act now in one step: "order now", "book Saturday", "call today", with a real deadline only if one exists | Burying the offer under brand storytelling |

How the stage maps to Studio's aims (`AIM_ANGLES`) and to campaign phases:

| Stage | Studio aims and campaign phases |
|---|---|
| Unaware / problem aware | Reach, share, a campaign's tease phase |
| Solution aware | Educate, compare, a launch's reveal phase |
| Product aware | Trust, proof, the sell phase |
| Most aware | Convert, a last-days phase (only with a real end date) |

**Market sophistication** is Schwartz's second axis: how many similar claims this market has
already heard. When every rival says "best quality", a bigger claim does not help.

- A *mechanism* does: how it works and why that is different.
- So does an *identification*: who it is for.

This is why section 1.7 exists.

## 1.2 Hook families

The hook is the first line of a caption, the headline on a picture, or the first 1.5 seconds of
a video. Each family below is a way to earn the second look. The "Real data?" column marks
families that are dishonest without facts from the business:

- **Yes:** the family needs facts the business gave, such as a number, a review, a date or a
  result. Without them, skip the family.
- **No:** it works from the brief alone.

| # | Family | How (one line) | Suits | Real data? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Call-out** | Name the exact person or situation: "Landlords with a 1970s boiler…" | Local services, professional services, B2B, niche products | No |
| 2 | **Pain in their words** | Say the problem the way the customer says it, not the way the industry does | Trades, clinics, SaaS, cleaning, repair | No |
| 3 | **Result first** | Open on the finished outcome, then rewind to how | Salons, trades, design, renovation, food | Yes (must be the business's real work) |
| 4 | **Number** | A specific, true figure: years, jobs, minutes, price | Any | Yes |
| 5 | **Question that stings** | A question the reader can't answer "no" to without thinking | Professional services, education, health (no fear) | No |
| 6 | **Myth or mistake** | "Most people [do X]. It's why [Y]." Correct a common error | Experts, trades, fitness, finance (no advice claims), software | No (the correction must be true) |
| 7 | **Process reveal** | Show the inside: how it is made, cleaned, fixed, cooked, built | Makers, food, trades, studios, manufacturers | No (show only the real process) |
| 8 | **Pattern interrupt** | A visual or line that doesn't fit the feed: odd angle, slow-mo, silence, a surprise object | Products, venues, game studios, launches | No |
| 9 | **Comparison / this or that** | Two options side by side, with the trade-off stated fairly | Products with variants, menus, plans, services with tiers | No (never a named rival) |
| 10 | **Story opening** | First line of a short true story: "Tuesday, 7am, the call came in…" | Founders, trades, venues, clinics, agencies | Yes (it must have happened) |
| 11 | **Social proof lead** | Open with a real review line, a real count, a real client (with permission) | Any with real proof | Yes |
| 12 | **Offer lead** | Lead with the offer itself: what, price, condition | Retail, restaurants, salons, events (most aware) | Yes (the real offer) |
| 13 | **Curiosity gap (honest)** | Promise one specific thing the piece really delivers: "The one setting that fixes blurry phone photos" | Tips, software, education, products with a trick | No (the payoff must be in the piece) |
| 14 | **Seasonal or moment tie-in** | Link to a real date, season, local event or trend the brand fits | Venues, retail, food, events | No (the date must be real; trend via Trend Radar only) |
| 15 | **Demonstration** | Show it working in the first frame: the stain lifting, the app doing the task, the door fitting | Products, SaaS, tools, trades | No (the demo must be real or a faithful depiction of the real product) |
| 16 | **Empathy / "you're not alone"** | Acknowledge a feeling without exploiting it | Health, therapy, education, financial services | No (never fear, shame or negative self-image) |

Hooks Mellox never writes:

- invented urgency ("only 2 left!" with no stock data);
- fake breaking news;
- fake notifications, chats or system dialogs;
- "doctors hate this";
- body-shaming openers;
- exploiting death, illness or debt to push a quick action. Google's misrepresentation policy
  bans this pressure tactic by name.

## 1.3 Offer construction for small businesses, without invented urgency

An offer is what someone gets, what it costs and under what terms. Most small-business posts fail
because the offer is vague, not because it is weak.

The value equation in the MIT `offers` skill is a useful lens:

- Value rises with the **outcome** and the **reader's belief they will get it**.
- Value falls with **waiting** and **effort**.

Price is the comparison, not the value. Often the honest way to make an offer stronger is to make
it **faster** or **easier**, not cheaper.

### The five parts of a clear offer

| Part | Rule | Required? |
|---|---|---|
| **What** | The exact thing: product, service, package, quantity, size. "Deep clean, 2-bed flat", not "cleaning services" | Always |
| **Price or value** | A real price, a real saving against a real previous price, or what is included. No "worth $X" without a real comparable | Always when the business has given a price; else describe what's included |
| **Condition** | Who it's for, where, minimum order, booking needed, area served | When one exists |
| **Deadline** | Only a real one: end date, last booking day, limited batch with a real count, a season | **Only if the business stated it** |
| **Risk reversal** | Only a real one: refund window, free re-do, free quote, cancel anytime, trial | **Only if the business offers it** |

Optional extras, all only when real:

- a bonus that is really included;
- a payment plan;
- a name for the package;
- a reason for the offer ("we're new on this street", "end of season").

A real reason makes even a small discount believable.

### What makes an offer feel clear

- **One offer per piece.** Two offers halve both.
- **Numbers stay together.** The price and what it buys sit in the same line or block.
- **The condition is near the offer**, not hidden in the caption's end.
- **The deadline is a date**, not "soon" or "limited time". If there is no date, don't imply one.
- **The CTA matches the offer.** A booking offer gets "book", a product gets "order" or "shop".
- **It reads the same on the picture, in the caption and on the landing page.** Google and TikTok
  both reject a mismatch between ad and destination.

### Good and bad examples (generic businesses)

| Business | Bad (vague or dishonest) | Good (clear and honest) | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bakery | "Amazing deals this week only!!" | "Sourdough loaf £4. Two for £7 until Sunday 30 Nov." | What, price and deadline are all real and specific |
| Plumber | "Best plumber in town, call now before it's too late" | "Boiler service £79, same-week visits in North Leeds. Free re-visit if the fault returns in 30 days." | Condition (area) and a real risk reversal; no superlative |
| Hair salon | "Transform your look! Limited spots!!!" (no count) | "Cut and blow-dry £45, Tuesdays and Wednesdays. Book online, cancel free up to 24h before." | Real condition and real flexibility; no fake scarcity |
| SaaS | "Revolutionary AI platform, 10x your productivity" | "Send invoices in 2 clicks. Free for 14 days, no card needed." | A concrete job and a real trial; no hype words |
| Accountant | "Save thousands on tax!" | "Self-assessment filed for £150 fixed fee. Book by 15 January for this year's deadline." | Fixed price, and a real external deadline (the tax date) |
| Restaurant | "Best food ever, don't miss out" | "Set lunch, 2 courses £16, Mon–Fri 12–3." | Exact offer and hours |
| Gym / studio | "Get your dream body in 30 days" | "First class free. 45-minute beginner Pilates, Saturdays 9am." | No body promise; a real, low-risk entry |
| Online shop | "Only 3 left!!" (no stock data) | "New colour: forest green. Free returns within 30 days." | Uses real policy instead of fake scarcity |
| Real estate agent | "Prices are skyrocketing, buy before it's too late" | "Free valuation this month: a written estimate within 48 hours." | No market scare; a concrete, real service promise |
| Event venue | "Selling fast!" (no data) | "Doors 7pm Fri 12 Dec. Tickets £15, £12 before 1 Dec." | The early price and its date are real; no "selling fast" without numbers |

Banned offer words without backing:

- "limited time" (with no time);
- "only X left" (with no stock);
- "worth $X" (with no comparable);
- "guaranteed" (with no stated terms);
- "lowest price", "best", "#1" (with no source);
- "free" when the item is not free (hidden fees count as dishonest pricing under Google's policy).

## 1.4 Objection handling per business kind

Every reader brings a silent "but". A piece answers **one** objection well; a campaign answers
them across pieces. The answer must use the business's real facts. Where there is no fact, pick
a different objection.

| Kind | Typical objections | Honest answers that work (pick the one the business can back) |
|---|---|---|
| **Physical product** | "Is it worth the price?" "Will it fit / suit me?" "Is it good quality?" "What if I don't like it?" "How long does delivery take?" | Show the material and the making close up. Show it in real use at true scale. Give the size or fit guide. State the returns policy. Give real delivery times. Compare cost-per-use honestly. Use a real customer photo with consent. |
| **Local service** (trades, cleaning, salon, repair, pet care) | "Can I trust a stranger in my home?" "Will they turn up?" "Hidden costs?" "Will it be done right?" | Show the named team member (with consent) and their real van or uniform. Give a fixed price or a free quote. Share an arrival window. Name the insurance or accreditation they hold. Show finished jobs from their own portfolio. Explain the re-do policy. |
| **Professional service** (accountant, lawyer, consultant, agency, clinic) | "Do they understand my situation?" "Is it expensive?" "What exactly do I get?" "How long will it take?" | Use a call-out to the exact client type. List the deliverables. Give a fixed fee or the fee structure. Show the process step by step with a timeline. Name the credentials held. Share a case study with permission. Offer a free first call. |
| **Digital / SaaS / app** | "Is it hard to set up?" "Will it work with what I use?" "Will I be locked in?" "Is my data safe?" | Show a screen demo of the real product doing the job in seconds. List the real integrations. Explain the trial, cancel-anytime terms and the free tier. Say where data is stored, only as the business states it. Give setup time from real onboarding. |
| **Venue** (restaurant, café, bar, hotel, gym, event space) | "Is it nice inside?" "Is it busy or quiet?" "Can I get a table?" "Is it worth the trip?" "Is it for people like me?" | Show a real walk-through of the room. Give hours and the best times. Explain the booking route. Share real menu items and prices. Show the location and parking. Share a real event schedule. Show real guests only with consent; never AI people posing as guests. |
| **Education / courses** | "Will I actually finish it?" "Is it for my level?" "Who teaches it?" | Show a real lesson clip. Give the level and time per week. Show the teacher (real, named). Explain the refund window. Show a real student outcome with permission. |
| **Events** | "Is it worth the ticket?" "Who else is going?" "Logistics?" | Share the real line-up or agenda. Show real footage from last time. Give the venue, date and time, access and price tiers. |

Patterns that cut across all kinds:

- **Feel, felt, found, honestly**: acknowledge the doubt, then show what the business really does
  about it.
- **Make the next step smaller.** Turn "buy" into a free quote, sample, trial, first class or
  tasting.
- **Specificity beats reassurance.** "Arrive 8–10am, text 30 min before" beats "reliable
  service".
- **Never answer an objection with a fake.** That means no invented reviews, made-up guarantees,
  or "as seen on" for press that never happened.

## 1.5 Calls to action

### By objective

These map onto Studio's ad objectives (`src/lib/studio/ad-objectives.ts`).

| Objective | CTA verbs that fit | Platform button (Meta / TikTok style) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sell** | Shop, Order, Buy, Get yours | Shop now, Buy now, Order now | Name the product. Add the real price if given |
| **Leads** | Get a quote, Get the guide, Check availability, Get your estimate | Get quote, Sign up, Learn more, Download | Say what they get and how fast. Never ask for more than the form really needs |
| **Bookings** | Book, Reserve, Pick a time | Book now | Give the day or slot if real ("Saturdays") |
| **Calls** | Call, Talk to us, Call for a free quote | Call now | Say when lines are open. Never fake an "urgent callback" |
| **Visits** (store, venue) | Visit, Come by, Find us, Get directions | Get directions, Learn more | Give the area or landmark and opening hours |
| **App installs** | Download, Get the app, Try it free | Install now, Download | Say what the app does in a few words first |
| **Follows** | Follow for…, Join us for… | Follow (organic) | Promise the specific value of following ("weekly fixes for…") |
| **Saves / shares** | Save this for…, Send this to someone who… | n/a (organic) | Only when the piece has lasting use: tips, lists, checklists |
| **Messages** | Message us, Ask us on WhatsApp, DM "menu" | Send message, Send WhatsApp message | Say what happens next ("we reply within the hour", only if true) |
| **Event** | Get tickets, Save your spot, RSVP | Get tickets, Sign up | Include the date and place |

### By placement

| Placement | Rules |
|---|---|
| **Caption** | Put the CTA in the last line, or the first line for most-aware offers. Use one CTA. A link works on Facebook and LinkedIn; on Instagram and TikTok say "link in bio" (Mellox's `linkRule`). Keep the hashtags after the CTA. |
| **Picture button** (drawn on the image) | Use 1–3 words, verb first, and one only. It is a design element, not a working button. It must never look like a real UI control that does nothing: TikTok bans non-working interactive elements. It should match the platform button chosen in Ads Manager. |
| **Link in bio** | Say what the reader will find there ("menu and booking in bio"). The bio link must lead there. Never "link in bio" for a link that doesn't exist. |
| **Click-to-call** | Give the hours. Put the number in text only if the business gave it. The CTA promises a person or a clear outcome ("free quote by phone"). |
| **WhatsApp / DM** | Suggest the first message ("Send 'SAT' to check Saturday slots") so the step feels tiny. Only promise reply times the business stated. |
| **Video end frame** | Show the logo, one line of offer or promise, and the CTA as text. Hold it for 1.5–2 s. Keep it inside the platform's safe zone, which keeps key content away from the UI edges. |
| **Search ad (RSA)** | At least one headline carries the CTA. Every headline must read alone in any order. 30 characters per headline, 90 per description. |

## 1.6 Proof

### The proof ladder (strongest honest proof first)

Use the highest rung the business can really supply. Lower rungs are fine; skipping proof is
better than faking a rung.

| Rung | Proof | What it needs |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Live demonstration of the real thing**: the product working, the service being done, the software doing the task on screen | Real product or footage; or AI-depicted product that is faithful to the real one (never exaggerating results) |
| 2 | **The business's own before/after of real work** (where allowed; not weight loss, not cosmetic side-by-sides that break policy) | Real photos the business took, with client consent for anything personal |
| 3 | **Named customer testimonial or case study, quoted with permission** | Real review text, real name or the attribution they agreed to |
| 4 | **Verified rating or count from a real source**: star rating on a public platform, number of jobs, years trading, customers served | The real figure, as of a date, from the business |
| 5 | **Credentials**: licence, accreditation, insurance, certification, award really won | The real credential. Logos of accreditors only if the business may use them |
| 6 | **Clients or partners (B2B)**: logos or names of real clients | Permission to show them |
| 7 | **Press or media mentions** that really happened | The real outlet and article |
| 8 | **Specific process or material facts**: "hand-stitched", "Grade A oak", "same engineer each visit" | The business stated it |
| 9 | **Risk reversal**: guarantee, refund, free re-do, trial | The business offers it, with its terms |
| 10 | **The team and the place**: real people (consenting) and the real premises | Their photos or permission |

### Fake-proof blacklist

Mellox never makes these, whatever the brief says. The "Banned by" column names the policy that
covers the pattern. Where no policy names it outright, it still falls under the general
deception rules.

| Never make | Why | Banned by |
|---|---|---|
| **Fake reviews or testimonials**: invented quotes, star ratings, "customer says…" with no real customer | Deceives buyers; illegal in the US since Oct 2024 | US FTC rule on consumer reviews and testimonials (16 CFR 465), covering AI-written reviews from people who don't exist. Meta's prohibited commercial practices ("unauthorized endorsements"). Google misrepresentation ("unreliable claims") |
| **Fake chats, DMs, texts or emails** shown as real customer messages | Same as a fake testimonial in a different costume | FTC rule (fake testimonials). TikTok misleading content (non-working UI). Google misrepresentation |
| **Fake Google results, fake review-site screenshots, fake app-store ratings** | Implies a third party's verdict that doesn't exist; uses another brand's look | Google misrepresentation (implied false affiliation, manipulated media). FTC rule (fake social-media indicators) |
| **Fake press**: "as seen on" logos, fake headlines, fake news-article layouts | False endorsement and false affiliation | Google misrepresentation (false affiliation). TikTok (false endorsement). Meta (unauthorized endorsements) |
| **Invented statistics**: "97% of customers…", "3x faster" with no source | Unsupported claims | Google misrepresentation ("unreliable claims"). TikTok (exaggerated or absolute claims) |
| **AI-generated people posing as customers, staff or the founder** | Misrepresents who is speaking. TikTok requires AI media to be labelled | TikTok misleading content (AI disclosure, likeness). FTC rule (reviewers who don't exist). Mellox's own rule: Creator videos are clearly ads by fictional creators, never "our customer Sarah" |
| **Before/after of real people without consent** | Privacy and likeness | TikTok (private likeness removed). Meta (unauthorized endorsements) |
| **Weight-loss before/after side-by-sides; body-fat pinch close-ups; "perfect body" promises** | Feeds negative self-image | Meta Health and Wellness (no side-by-side for weight loss; no negative self-perception). TikTok weight management "Body image" section |
| **Cosmetic-treatment side-by-sides** (wrinkle injectables, fillers, anti-ageing) | Restricted | Meta Health and Wellness |
| **Before/after that exaggerates the result**, including AI-enhanced "after" pictures | False impression of results | TikTok misleading content (distorted before/after). Google (manipulated media) |
| **Fake countdowns and fake stock counts** | Fake scarcity; can mislead on price | Google (dishonest pricing). TikTok (promotion mismatch). The MIT `offers` skill lists it as trust-destroying |
| **Fake buttons, play icons, close icons, notification badges** drawn on the picture | Non-working interactive elements | TikTok misleading content |
| **Celebrity or public-figure likeness, or lookalikes** | False endorsement | TikTok (public figure likeness). Google (false affiliation). Mellox's `customCreatorProblem` |
| **Competitor names or logos in comparisons** | Mellox rule (Autopilot briefs, campaigns), plus legal risk | Mellox ADR rules. Google (impersonation) |

When the brief asks for one of these, Studio does three things:

1. Turns it into the nearest honest version: a real review the business pastes in, a process
   shot, a real number, a guarantee it really offers.
2. Says so in one line.
3. If nothing honest is available, leaves the proof out.

## 1.7 Mechanisms: sixty reasons a piece sells

A mechanism is one sentence that explains **why** a piece works. Writers use it as the spine of
a concept: pick one, then make the headline, picture and CTA serve it. Each mechanism is generic
and ≤140 characters. None needs invented data; those that need a real fact say "real".

### Products (12)

1. Puts the product at true scale in a hand, so the buyer stops guessing whether it fits their life.
2. Shows the material up close until texture does the talking, so quality is seen rather than claimed.
3. Opens the box the way the buyer will, so the moment of owning it is felt before paying.
4. Shows it used on an ordinary day, not a perfect one, so the reader sees themselves using it.
5. Divides the price by the uses in a real year, so the cost feels small beside the habit.
6. Shows the one problem it fixes in a single frame, so the reader instantly knows why it exists.
7. Lines up every colour or variant, so choosing feels fun instead of risky.
8. Reveals how it's made, step by step, so care becomes a reason to pay more.
9. Pairs it with what it's usually used with, so the buyer pictures the full setup and adds both.
10. Shows it after months of real use, so durability is proven by wear rather than promised.
11. Names who it's not for, so the right buyer trusts the claim about who it is for.
12. Puts the gift moment in frame (wrapping, handing over) so the buyer sees the reaction they'll get.

### Local services (12)

13. Shows the finished job beside the mess it replaced, so the price feels small next to the relief.
14. Introduces the real person who'll arrive at the door, so a stranger becomes a familiar face.
15. Shows the van, the uniform and the boot covers, so trust forms before the phone call.
16. States a fixed price for a common job, so fear of a surprise bill stops blocking the call.
17. Shows the tidy-up after the work, so the reader knows their home will be left as found.
18. Explains one warning sign the owner can check, so the expert earns trust before selling anything.
19. Names the streets or areas served, so locals feel the business is theirs and nearby.
20. Shows the arrival window and the "on my way" text, so reliability is seen, not claimed.
21. Films the hardest part of the job, so the reader sees why it's worth paying a pro.
22. Shows a real slot opening on the calendar (real), so booking feels easy and immediate.
23. Uses the season's real problem (frozen pipes, overgrown hedges), so the service lands on time.
24. Shows a pet or child-safe product being used, so the household's first worry is answered.

### Professional services (12)

25. Names the exact client and the exact moment they need help, so the right reader feels seen.
26. Turns a confusing process into three labelled steps, so the next step feels small and safe.
27. States the fixed fee and what it includes, so cost stops being the reason not to ask.
28. Shows the real deliverable (report, plan, design), so an invisible service becomes a thing.
29. Corrects one common mistake with a clear fix, so expertise is proven by being useful.
30. Puts a real date on the calendar (a filing deadline), so acting now has a genuine reason.
31. Shows the founder explaining one idea plainly, so the reader meets the mind they'd hire.
32. Shares a client result with permission and context, so outcome claims rest on a real case.
33. Shows the first call is free and short, so reaching out costs nothing but ten minutes.
34. Lists what they won't do, so promises on what they will do feel more believable.
35. Shows the credential and what it means for the client, so letters become reassurance.
36. Compares doing it alone with having help, honestly, so the reader weighs time, not just fee.

### Digital, SaaS and apps (12)

37. Shows the job done on the real screen in seconds, so the claim "it's easy" proves itself.
38. Puts the old way and the new way side by side on screen, so time saved is visible.
39. Starts on the result (sent invoice, booked slot) and rewinds, so the payoff comes first.
40. Shows the app on a phone in a real hand mid-task, so it feels like part of the day.
41. Lists the tools it already works with as logos (real), so switching feels safe.
42. Shows the free trial terms plainly, so trying costs nothing and nobody feels tricked.
43. Walks through the first five minutes after sign-up, so setup fear disappears.
44. Shows one feature solving one named annoyance, so the reader remembers it by its job.
45. Shows the team behind the product (real), so software feels backed by people who answer.
46. Shows a real template or output the user gets, so value is concrete before they sign up.
47. Uses a "you've been doing this manually" call-out, so wasted hours become obvious.
48. Shows the update log or new feature with a date (real), so the product feels alive.

### Venues (restaurants, cafés, hotels, gyms, event spaces) (12)

49. Walks through the door at eye level, so the reader feels what arriving is like.
50. Shows the busiest dish being made in the real kitchen, so appetite does the selling.
51. Shows the room at the time the reader would come, so the mood they want is the mood they see.
52. Pairs the offer with the day it runs (real), so the visit gets a slot in the reader's week.
53. Shows the table, seat or bed the reader would get, so booking feels concrete.
54. Films the small ritual (latte art, key handover, warm towel), so care becomes the reason to come.
55. Shows a real event happening (with consent), so the place feels alive and worth the trip.
56. Answers "where do I park?" or "how do I get there?", so a practical worry stops the no.
57. Shows the menu with real prices, so nobody fears the bill before they sit down.
58. Shows the view or the light at its real best hour, so the reader plans the visit for it.
59. Shows the staff by name doing their craft (consenting), so the visit feels personal.
60. Ties the venue to a real local moment (match day, festival, first snow), so now feels right.

# Part 2: Short video ads (9:16, 6–30 s)

## 2.0 What the platforms and guides agree on

- **Vertical 9:16, at least 720p, key content inside the safe zone.** Keep text, logo and
  product away from the top 10–15% and bottom 20–25%, and from the right edge where the
  platform's buttons sit. *TikTok creative best practices.*
- **The message comes early.** TikTok's guidance:
  - put the core proposition in the first 3 seconds;
  - land the hook within the first 6 seconds;
  - use sound;
  - use a native, not over-polished feel;
  - feature creators, staff or customers;
  - add text overlays for context;
  - end on a clear CTA.
- **Short wins for in-feed.** Meta's video format page recommends 15 seconds as a general
  ceiling. Treat 6–15 s as the default and 30 s as the long form for stories that need it (a
  service call-out, a walk-through).
- **Plan longer videos as shots.** Most generation models make 4–15 s per clip. A 30 s ad is
  three to six shots joined. In Studio, story kinds chain (each clip opens on the last frame),
  and mood kinds dissolve (ADR-0041).
- **Assume sound off; reward sound on.** On-screen text carries the message. Voice and music add
  feeling. Mellox's soundtrack is a voiceover of the storyboard's words plus a ducked bed (-14
  LUFS).
- **Test difference, not tweaks.** TikTok suggests 3–5 creatives per ad group and favours big
  differences. Mellox should offer different *arcs* (below) as versions, not colour tweaks.

## 2.1 Arcs

Each arc gives beats with start times for 8 s, 15 s and 30 s cuts. Times are seconds from the
start. "Text" means on-screen words, drawn by code, never by the video model. Every arc ends
on a **held end frame**:

- 1.5–2 s long;
- shows the logo, one line and the CTA;
- the opening frame comes from the post where there is one (`openingFrameForVideo`).

### Arc 1: Product premium reveal

- **Business kinds:** any physical product with a strong form: cosmetics, drinks, gadgets,
  jewellery, bags, watches, packaged food.
- **Opening (0–1.5 s):** darkness or a soft void. A sliver of rim light catches one edge of the
  product, or the product rises or turns into the light. One word of text at most.
- **Beats:**
  - **8 s:**
    - 0 light catches the edge;
    - 2 slow turn reveals the face and label;
    - 5 close-up of one detail (texture, cap, clasp);
    - 6.5 end frame.
  - **15 s:**
    - 0 edge light;
    - 2.5 rise or turn to full view;
    - 5 detail 1;
    - 8 detail 2 or variant line-up;
    - 11 hero pose with the product name;
    - 13 end frame.
  - **30 s:**
    - 0 edge light;
    - 3 full reveal;
    - 7 material story (macro);
    - 12 variants;
    - 17 in a hand for scale;
    - 21 in its world (bathroom shelf, desk, table);
    - 26 hero plus line;
    - 28 end frame.
- **Proof:** the detail shots are the proof (craft, material). A real rating or award goes as
  text on the hero pose, only if real.
- **End frame:** product centred or lower third, with the name, one benefit line, the CTA and
  the price if given.
- **Text rules:** 2–4 words per card, one card on screen at a time, never over the label.
- **Sound:** low swell or pulse, a soft hit on the reveal. Voiceover optional (one line).
- **Tintora note:** their product-commercial samples are this arc. Examples:
  - a handbag hanging from a large gold ring before draped silk;
  - a face-wash tube on a stone in rippling water, with tea leaves and wood props.

  Learn from them: one product, a world built from its ingredients or materials, slow camera
  moves, and the label kept exact. Mellox's equivalent is the product hero (ADR-0040) carried
  into motion.

## 2.2 Rules for AI video models

### What fails, and the house rules that avoid it

| Failure | Why it happens | House rule |
|---|---|---|
| **Readable text in footage** | Models garble letters, and Veo can burn in subtitles by itself | Never ask a video model to draw words. Code draws all text (`overlayHookText`, captions, end frames). End Veo prompts by asking for no subtitles, captions or overlays |
| **Labels and logos drift** | The model redraws the product each frame | Start from a reference or first frame of the real product. State that the product, label and colours stay unchanged in every shot. Keep the label facing the camera only in slow moves. Clear the logo from the opening frame unless the person asked (`wantsLogoInVideo`) |
| **Crowds and many people** | Entities merge, faces melt, limbs multiply | At most 3 main subjects per shot (Creatify's limit). Say the exact count. Use "empty" or "a few people in soft focus" rather than "busy". No invented crowds for venues |
| **Hands** | Finger counts and grips break, especially holding small objects | Keep hands simple: one hand, one object, a slow move. Describe the grip. Prefer the object resting or rotating to fiddly manipulation |
| **Physics** | Liquids, cloth and collisions go wrong in weak models | Use one physical event per shot. Pick the model that is good at it (Veo for pours and cloth). Avoid stacked interactions (pour + stir + steam) |
| **Faces and identity across shots** | Each clip invents a new person | Use a reference image of the creator (`stockCreatorAsset`) on multi-reference models, the same wardrobe line in every clip, and chained clips that open on the last frame |
| **Rushed or mushy action** | Too much asked in one clip | One main action per segment. Use timestamped segments for multi-beat clips. Use degree adverbs ("slowly", "deliberately") |
| **Negatives ignored** | Models attend to the nouns in "no X" | Describe what is there ("an empty counter") instead of what isn't |
| **Over-cinematic look on UGC** | Veo defaults to film polish | For Creator videos, ask for phone-camera texture and natural indoor light. Avoid words like "cinematic", "stunning" or "8k" in UGC prompts (Arcads' Seedance guide bans such words) |
| **Letterboxing or wrong frame** | Wrong ratio from the reference | Use an opening frame in the video's shape (`openingFrameFit`). QA rejects bars (`looksLetterboxed`) |
| **Real people or brands** | Likeness and false endorsement | No celebrities, no real people without consent, no other brands' logos. Creators are original, fictional people |

### Consistency lines (use them in every multi-clip plan)

- **Bible in every clip:** the same subject description, wardrobe, product description, palette,
  lighting and lens in each clip prompt (`clipPrompt` already does this).
- **Product lock:** say that the product from the reference stays visually unchanged: shape,
  label, colours and proportions.
- **Wardrobe and set lock:** the same outfit and the same room across cuts, unless the arc
  changes place.
- **Light lock:** one key-light direction and colour temperature for the whole video.
- **Chain or dissolve:** story arcs chain (last frame → next first frame). Mood arcs run in
  parallel and dissolve.
- **Count lock:** state the number of people and objects in each shot.

## 2.3 QA: why video ads fail and how to judge from three frames plus the script

A reviewer (a person, or the `studio.video.qa` judge) gets three frames:

- **F1** at about 0.5 s (the hook);
- **F2** at the middle (the body);
- **F3** at the last second (the end frame).

They also get the script or storyboard text. Each check below can be answered from that alone.

| Failure | What it looks like | Check from 3 frames + script | Fail if |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Weak hook** | F1 is a logo, a blank sky, a slow fade or a generic stock-like scene | Does F1 alone show a person, a problem, a product in action or a surprising image? Does the first script line name a person, problem or result within ~8 words? | F1 shows no subject; the first line is a greeting, the brand name or filler ("Introducing…", "Hey guys") |
| **Product or service unclear** | You can't tell what is sold | Is the product or the service's work visible and recognisable in F2 or F3? Is it named in on-screen text or script by F3? | No frame shows the product or the work; the name appears nowhere; the label is unreadable or changed (compare with the catalogue photo) |
| **Message unclear** | Three ideas fighting | Can you write the message in one sentence from the script? Does F2's text support that sentence? | The script makes more than one main claim; on-screen text contradicts the voiceover; the claim isn't in Brand DNA or the catalogue |
| **No or weak CTA** | Ends on a pretty shot with no next step | Does F3 show one CTA verb plus how (link, call, book, visit)? | No CTA; two competing CTAs; a CTA that doesn't match the objective |
| **Pacing** | Too slow (one shot for 10 s) or too busy (cuts every 0.3 s) | Script word count versus duration: about 2–2.5 spoken words per second; ≤ 6 words per on-screen card. Shot count versus duration: about 1 shot per 2–4 s for 15 s ads | The script exceeds about 2.5 words/s; cards over 6 words; a 15 s ad with one static shot (unless a deliberate oner) |
| **Visual defects** | Melting hands, extra fingers, garbled text, warped label, bars | Inspect F1–F3 for hands, text, label and edges. Run `looksLetterboxed` | Any garbled text in the footage; a changed label; a broken hand in a frame where hands matter; bars |
| **Unsafe area** | Text under the platform UI | Are the text and logo inside the safe zone in F1 and F3? | Key text in the bottom ~20% or right ~15% |
| **Honesty** | Fake proof or exaggerated result | Does any frame or script line show a review, number, before/after, press or person-as-customer? Is each one backed in Brand DNA or the brief? | Anything on the 1.6 blacklist; a result shown beyond what the product does |
| **Off-brand** | Colours, type or tone don't match the brand | Compare F2's palette and F3's type with `dna.look` | The end frame doesn't use the brand's colours or logo correctly |
| **Sound-off failure** | The message only in voice | Read F1–F3 text only: is the offer and CTA still clear? | The message is unclear without audio |

Scoring suggestion, consistent with Studio's one-redo rule:

- Hook, clarity and honesty are **hard**: one redo, then the job fails.
- Pacing, safe area and brand are **soft**: one redo if fixable; otherwise deliver with a note
  only if the person's own footage caused it.
- A failed check is never turned into a warning (ADR-0041).
